> Angle: the voice clone scare, one real case, then what The Watch does.
> Length is tuned for LinkedIn's "see more" cut. The hook has to survive the

---

A finance worker joined a routine video call with the CFO and a few colleagues.

Everyone on that call was a deepfake except him.

He approved 15 transfers. $25 million, gone. The company was Arup, a 18,000
person global engineering firm with a real security budget and a real security
team. Hong Kong police reported it in early 2024.

Here is the part that should worry every small business owner I know.

Arup had defenses and still lost. Most of my clients here have a bookkeeper, a
shared inbox, and a rule that lives in somebody's head.

The scam almost always runs in three moves:

1. Someone registers a domain one letter off from yours. Your name with an extra
letter, or a different ending, or with the word "invoice" bolted on.
2. That domain emails your bookkeeper something that looks completely normal. An
invoice. A change of bank details.
3. Then the phone rings, and it is your voice, because 30 seconds of you talking
is now enough to clone it. Urgent. Confidential. Today.

BEC scams like this took $2.77 billion off US businesses in 2024 alone, across
21,442 reported cases. That is the FBI's own number, and it is second only to
investment fraud.

So I built beware.dog, for the businesses too small to have a security team.

Every week it scans for domains registered to impersonate yours and tells you
within one business day when a new one appears. Once a month it drills the
people who can actually send a payment, by email and by phone. Then you get one
page telling you what it found.

That is it. It does not read your email, it does not touch your files, and it
will not pretend to stop ransomware. I would rather tell you exactly where it
stops.

If you want to see what is already registered against your business, reply
"scan" and I will run yours and send you the result. Free, no pitch attached.

beware.dog

Question for the owners here, especially anyone who pays invoices by email.

Has anyone had a supplier email turn out to be fake? I keep hearing versions of
the same story: an invoice arrives that looks completely normal, the bank
details are slightly different, and by the time anyone calls to check, the wire
has cleared.

The setup for it is usually the same. Somebody registers a domain that looks
like the business, one letter off or a different ending, and emails the person
who handles payments from it. Now they can also clone a voice off a short clip,
so the follow up call sounds like the owner.

I run a small service called beware.dog that watches for this. It checks weekly
whether anyone has registered a lookalike of your domain, runs a monthly
practice run on whoever handles your payments, and sends a one page summary.
It is $149 a month and you can cancel anytime.

But mostly I am offering this: if you want, send me your business domain and I
will run the scan for free and tell you what is already registered against you.
No charge and no pitch. It takes me a few minutes and I think more owners here
should see their own result.

Happy to answer anything about how these scams actually work.
